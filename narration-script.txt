c01s03 Chapter 1: Cognition leaves clues, not a direct recording.
Cognitive psychology studies processes such as attention, memory, language and reasoning. Researchers infer these processes from patterns of behavior and other measurements.

Maya’s repair is an observation. A claim about her memory adds an explanation. The same action could follow several different processes.

A useful model predicts what should change when a relevant condition changes. An attractive story about a mind becomes a stronger explanation when its predictions survive tests.

c01s03b Chapter 1: Compare predictions, then change a condition.
To investigate Maya’s repair, compare familiar and unfamiliar equipment, and compare helpful with unhelpful labels. Decide beforehand how each explanation predicts performance.

A controlled experiment changes a proposed cause and measures an outcome. Random assignment helps separate that change from existing differences between people. Practice and task order also need attention.

A faster repair could reflect experience, a useful cue or a willingness to make mistakes. Accuracy and response time together tell a fuller story than speed alone.

c01s04 Chapter 1: A measure has more than one explanation.
Imagine that Sam answers a fault checklist faster after practice, but misses more faults. The shorter response time does not establish better reasoning. Sam may simply have changed how much evidence is needed before answering.

Response time and accuracy depend on the task, instructions and strategy. Researchers compare patterns across conditions rather than treating one score as a complete description of a person.

Brain measurements add evidence, but an active brain region is not a unique label for one thought. Stronger explanations combine suitable methods and competing predictions.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Laboratory tasks isolate particular demands so explanations can be tested. That control can also limit how directly a result applies to a noisy repair café.

A within-person comparison can reduce some differences between participants, but practice, fatigue and order effects remain. A between-person comparison needs sufficiently comparable groups. Replication and converging measures strengthen confidence; one successful demonstration does not settle a theory.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Perception organizes sensory information.
Sensation begins with physical stimulation and sensory systems. Perception involves interpreting that information as meaningful objects, events or patterns. The distinction is useful even though the processes interact.

Sam uses the visible strokes on the label and prior experience with familiar parts. When the print is incomplete, that prior knowledge can help recognition or favor the wrong interpretation.

Perception is usually useful, but it is not a photographic copy. To check the label, improve the signal and compare it with an independent record.

c02s03b Chapter 2: Context can make an ambiguous mark seem clear.
An incomplete shape may look like a letter among letters and a number among numbers. The physical input can stay similar while the surrounding information changes the most plausible interpretation.

At the café, Sam expects a familiar fuse code. Maya expects a different category of part. Their expectations supply context, not additional strokes on the label.

Changing the lighting or consulting the parts register can provide new evidence. Merely feeling more certain after discussing the first reading does not improve the original signal.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Detection and willingness to say yes differ.
Suppose volunteers check whether a quiet device is making a warning sound. Some faint sounds are real warnings; others are background noise.

Signal detection theory distinguishes sensitivity from the decision criterion. A cautious volunteer may report more possible warnings, producing more hits and more false alarms without hearing more accurately.

To compare performance, record both signal-present and signal-absent trials. A high number of yes responses alone does not tell you who distinguishes the sounds best.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Perceptual demonstrations show that interpretation can depend on context. They do not establish that every disagreement is an illusion, or that seeing is generally unreliable.

Signal detection measures require defined signals, noise conditions and response categories. An everyday judgment with no agreed reference cannot automatically be given a sensitivity score. The café examples illustrate distinctions rather than reporting an experiment.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Attention selects information for a task.
Attention helps prioritize information relevant to a current task. Selection is useful because competing demands cannot all receive the same depth of processing.

Leo’s tool count draws attention to items and totals. The visitor may enter his visual field without becoming part of what he consciously reports. That does not show whether the visitor was physically hard to see.

Distinguish the available information from the information selected for the task. A missed event can reflect the task, the signal, expectations or several of these together.

c03s03b Chapter 3: An unexpected event can go unnoticed.
Inattentional blindness describes failing to notice an unexpected visible event while attention is engaged by another task. Researchers often compare reports after participants concentrate on a specified activity.

Leo’s missed visitor is compatible with that idea, but the story does not establish it. Perhaps the sightline was blocked or the arrival was unusually quiet.

To test an attention account, compare comparable arrivals while the counting demand changes and visibility stays similar. Asking people to watch specifically for visitors creates a different task from noticing an unexpected arrival.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Switching tasks has a cost.
When Sam interrupts the tool count, Leo must hold his place, answer a question and return to the counting rule. Some interruptions are helpful, but switching can carry time and error costs.

Executive control refers to processes that help maintain goals and regulate behavior. It is a family of functions, not one fixed supply that explains every lapse.

A practical test might compare a quiet counting period with a scheduled interruption, while recording counting errors and visitor reports. Reducing interruptions is a hypothesis to assess, not proof that people can eliminate every attentional limit.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Laboratory demonstrations show that substantial events can be missed under particular attention demands. The proportion missed varies with the task, event, similarity and expectations. A demonstration does not measure someone’s general care or intelligence.

Interruption studies also depend on what the tasks require. Some practiced activities overlap more easily than two unfamiliar tasks drawing on the same processes. Avoid treating every pair of activities as equally demanding.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Working memory supports thinking in progress.
Working memory helps keep information available while we use it. Following instructions can require holding the next step, remembering what has already happened and updating the plan.

Maya’s task changes when Sam revises an earlier instruction. The difficulty is not just remembering separate words; she must maintain an ordered goal and replace part of it.

Working-memory models differ in how they describe storage and control. They agree that performance depends on the task and interference rather than unlimited short-term storage.

c04s03b Chapter 4: A meaningful chunk depends on knowledge.
A familiar sequence such as a common postcode can be treated as one meaningful unit by someone who knows it. The same characters may be several unfamiliar elements to another person.

Chunking can reduce the number of units that must be held separately. It relies on learned organization; adding spaces to an arbitrary list does not guarantee a useful chunk.

There is no single number of items that describes every person and task. Estimates depend on rehearsal, prior knowledge, modality, interference and how the experiment defines an item.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Move a memory demand into the environment.
The written lamp checklist lets Maya inspect the next step instead of repeatedly holding the entire sequence in mind. A movable marker can show where she paused.

This is cognitive offloading: using the environment to reduce an internal information-processing demand. The aid still needs to be accurate, available and understandable. An outdated checklist can introduce new errors.

Compare performance on similar tasks with and without the aid. Improvement on that task supports the usefulness of the aid; it does not show a broad increase in working-memory capacity.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Working-memory limits are well supported, but exact capacity estimates depend on the method and assumptions. A small estimated capacity is not a diagnosis or an intelligence score.

Learning a practiced task, using an external aid and changing a general cognitive capacity are different claims. Benefits from training are often strongest for trained or similar tasks; transfer to broad everyday abilities requires separate evidence.

c05s03 Chapter 5: Learning must survive the later task.
Encoding, storage and retrieval are useful distinctions in describing memory. A failure at test can have several sources: information may not have been learned well, retained, or accessible with the available cues.

Leo’s familiar notes provide many cues. A visitor’s new wording provides fewer of those same prompts. Recognition with the page open therefore does not guarantee unaided explanation.

c05s03b Chapter 5: Retrieve, then check what was missing.

Research often finds that attempting retrieval improves later retention compared with additional study under suitable conditions. An attempt can also expose a gap that rereading hides.

Retrieval is not automatically useful if errors remain uncorrected or the practice fails to address the intended skill. A memorized phrase can be recalled without understanding its application.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Space practice and vary useful examples.

Mixing relevant examples can require deciding which principle applies. That can feel harder than repeating one problem type, but may improve later discrimination in suitable tasks.

Spacing and interleaving are different manipulations. Neither guarantees far transfer. To assess understanding, ask Leo to explain a new situation and identify where the original rule no longer fits.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Retrieval practice and distributed practice have broad research support, but their effects depend on material, feedback, timing and the final assessment. A laboratory average is not a promised gain for every learner.

Immediate performance can improve while delayed retention remains weak. Compare methods with a meaningful delay and the same outcome. Claims about conceptual transfer need new examples, not only repetition of the study questions.

c06s03 Chapter 6: Remembering is a reconstruction.
Memory can preserve important features while also being influenced by knowledge, goals and later information. A schema helps organize what usually happens in a situation, but can also supply a plausible detail that was not present.

At the café, a familiar tool-case story may make the later description feel natural. That feeling does not identify where the color information came from.

Calling memory reconstructive does not mean all memories are false. It means confidence in a coherent account should be considered alongside its source and independent evidence.

c06s03b Chapter 6: Ask where a detail came from.
Source monitoring concerns how we judge whether information came from perception, imagination, conversation or another source. Those judgments can be mistaken.

Leo’s question contains the color red before Sam supplies it. Later, Sam may remember the detail without correctly remembering that it was introduced in the question.

Begin with an open invitation such as, “What do you remember near the door?” Record the account before discussing alternatives. An open question reduces one source of suggestion; it cannot guarantee complete accuracy.

c06s04 Chapter 6: Confidence and correctness need separate evidence.
An account can become easier to tell after rehearsal or discussion. Familiarity and social agreement may then increase confidence without adding independent evidence.

Confidence can be informative in some well-controlled identification procedures, particularly when recorded early and without contaminating feedback. It is not a universal certificate of accuracy.

For the café case, preserve Sam’s initial wording, note Leo’s question and inspect the photo’s timing and viewpoint. The photo is also evidence with limits: it may not show every moment or location.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Experiments on misleading post-event information show that later questions can influence reports of an earlier event. Effects vary with the event, delay, wording, source and test.

The café story is fictional and cannot diagnose a particular memory process. Neither a discrepancy nor confidence alone establishes deliberate deception, a false memory, or the correctness of an alternative account.

c07s03 Chapter 7: Words require context to become a message.
Understanding a sentence involves more than recognizing its words. Syntax helps specify relationships, semantics supplies meaning, and pragmatics concerns how context shapes what a speaker intends.

“The cabinet” has a familiar meaning, but there are two possible cabinets at the café. Sam and Maya rely on different assumptions about the shared situation.

Communication succeeds when the listener identifies a useful interpretation for the current purpose. The same sentence can be clear in one setting and ambiguous in another.

c07s03b Chapter 7: Shared knowledge can be assumed too quickly.
Maya has been working beside one cabinet all morning and assumes it is the obvious reference. Sam has just arrived through the entrance and treats the nearby cabinet as obvious.

Speakers and listeners use common ground, but can overestimate what the other person knows or has noticed. Successful reference sometimes requires updating that estimate.

A useful repair is specific: “Do you mean the cabinet beside the workbench?” Repeating the original sentence more confidently leaves the same ambiguity in place.

c07s04 Chapter 7: Check the interpretation, not just agreement.
Sam can say yes while picturing the wrong cabinet. Agreement is a conversational response, not a direct measurement of understanding.

For an important instruction, ask the listener to point to the intended location or describe the next step. Maya can then compare that interpretation with what she intended and clarify it.

A comprehension check should match the task and respect the listener. It is not an intelligence test. Language familiarity, hearing conditions, fatigue and the clarity of the instruction can all affect the exchange.

c07s05 Chapter 7: What does the evidence allow?
Psycholinguistic research uses tasks such as reading, listening, naming and conversational reference to examine language processing. A result from one task does not explain every communication difficulty.

Linguistic relativity concerns how language can influence aspects of thought. It does not require assuming that people are unable to think about anything their language does not name. Individual experience and task conditions matter.

c08s03 Chapter 8: A category helps organize, but can conceal variation.
Categories let us use prior knowledge when encountering a new object or problem. People can draw on typical features, remembered examples or explicit rules, depending on the task.

A familiar broken cable may become Leo’s typical repair problem. That makes similar cases easy to recognize, but can draw attention away from a fault with a different cause.

A typical example is not a definition of every member. Check which features are actually relevant to the decision rather than relying only on resemblance.

c08s03b Chapter 8: A useful test separates competing accounts.
Leo’s successful cable replacements are compatible with his rule, but other rules could predict the same examples. Perhaps inspection, rather than cable replacement, is the useful common step.

A discriminating test looks for a case where the accounts make different predictions. A repair completed successfully without replacing a cable would challenge the claim that replacement is necessary.

Testing should be safe and ethically appropriate. Do not create a dangerous failure merely to challenge an idea. Records or a low-risk simulation may provide the needed comparison.

c08s04 Chapter 8: Change the representation of the problem.
If Leo represents the task as “Which cable should I replace?” he has already restricted the possible solution. Representing it as “What prevents this device from working?” leaves other causes open.

Functional fixedness can make an object’s familiar use dominate possible alternatives. Reframing a goal, drawing relationships or listing constraints may reveal another route.

An insight can feel sudden even when prior attempts contributed. A successful trick on one puzzle does not establish a general method for every complex problem.

c08s05 Chapter 8: What does the evidence allow?
Reasoning tasks show that people often test favored rules in ways that provide limited discrimination. The pattern depends on instructions, content, goals and what alternatives are available.

Confirmation bias is a family of related tendencies, not a diagnosis from one answer. A fair evaluation asks which hypotheses were considered and whether the proposed evidence would actually distinguish them.

c09s03 Chapter 9: Ease of recall is not a denominator.
An availability heuristic uses how easily examples come to mind as a cue in judgment. That can be useful when recall tracks experience, but vividness, recency and publicity also affect recall.

Leo can remember the overheating battery immediately. To estimate how common battery faults are, he needs a defined set of comparable assessments and a count of all relevant outcomes.

A base rate describes a reference group. Choosing the wrong group can mislead even when the arithmetic is correct. Safety severity and frequency are also different questions.

c09s03b Chapter 9: A positive signal is not a certain conclusion.
Consider an invented screening example with one hundred devices. Ten have a fault. A test flags eight of those ten and also flags nine of the ninety devices without the fault.

Seventeen devices test positive, but only eight of those seventeen have the fault. The test’s ability to detect a fault is not the same as the chance that a positive device is faulty.

Natural frequencies keep the relevant groups visible. These numbers are fictional teaching values, not diagnostic advice or a claim about an actual repair test.

c09s04 Chapter 9: Compare equivalent descriptions.
A service can describe two failures in one hundred repairs as a two-percent failure rate or a ninety-eight-percent success rate. The descriptions emphasize different outcomes but refer to the same counts.

A fall from four failures to two per hundred is a reduction of two percentage points and a relative reduction of fifty percent. The starting rate and shared denominator are essential.

A considered decision also needs the seriousness, costs and alternatives. Equivalent arithmetic does not settle what outcome a person should value most.

c09s05 Chapter 9: What does the evidence allow?
Judgment research identifies predictable influences of availability, framing and prior probabilities in specified tasks. A shortcut can be efficient or poorly suited to a problem; labeling it does not determine the correct decision.

The device-screening counts are invented to illustrate conditional probability. The uncertainty of real tests, the population sampled and the costs of different errors require additional evidence.

c10s03 Chapter 10: Monitor a judgment, then control the next step.
Metacognition concerns how people monitor and regulate their own cognitive activity. A feeling of knowing can guide a decision to study, answer or seek help.

Monitoring can be informative, but it relies on cues that sometimes mislead. Familiar wording may feel like understanding even when a person cannot explain the principle independently.

Calibration compares confidence with actual performance across suitable judgments. It cannot be inferred from whether one answer sounds modest or certain.

c10s03b Chapter 10: Use a check that supplies new evidence.
Leo can predict how well he will explain a procedure, close the notes and answer a practical question after a delay. Then he can compare the explanation with an accurate reference.

A new example tests whether he can select and apply the relevant principle rather than repeat familiar wording. A useful feedback record distinguishes factual errors, missing reasons and unjustified certainty.

One mismatch is information about that task. Several comparable judgments are needed before describing a stable calibration pattern.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Combine the course without explaining everything at once.
Return to the café: a person misses a visitor, forgets a step or misreads an instruction. The course offers possible processes, not automatic labels for the cause.

Begin with the task and observation. Consider attention, memory, language, prior knowledge or decision strategy where they generate different predictions. Choose a respectful and safe comparison that could distinguish them.

Keep any conclusion proportional to the evidence. A useful explanation specifies what it predicts, what remains uncertain and what additional information would change it.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
Metacognitive accuracy varies with the task, feedback and the cues available when people judge. Familiarity and confidence can sometimes be useful and sometimes misleading.

The course’s exercises provide practice in explanation and evidence checking. Completing pages or reporting confidence is not proof of mastery, a general reasoning gain or effective transfer to every setting. A delayed new case provides a more informative next check.
